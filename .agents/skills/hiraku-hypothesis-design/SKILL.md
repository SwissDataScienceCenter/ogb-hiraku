# Hiraku hypothesis design

Use this procedure when a user wants to turn a research question, evidence gap,
or candidate idea into hypotheses and an experiment design. Read relevant
Hiraku context and prior results first. Use the literature-review skill or a
saved literature synthesis when the task depends on published evidence. Keep
source-supported findings distinct from interpretation and assumptions.

## Procedure

1. **Generate.** Restate the research question, target population or setting,
   and practical constraints. Generate a small, diverse set of plausible,
   falsifiable candidate hypotheses. Vary the mechanism or intervention where
   evidence permits; avoid cosmetic rewordings and unsupported novelty claims.
   Link each candidate to its rationale and supporting evidence, labeling
   assumptions and inferences.
2. **Critique.** Examine each candidate independently. Ask what observation
   would falsify it, whether the proposed mechanism is plausible, what evidence
   is missing, what confounders or alternative explanations could account for
   the result, and whether the claim is measurable and feasible. Record the
   strongest objection and the main uncertainty for each candidate.
3. **Compare.** Compare candidates against the same criteria: explanatory
   value, evidence fit, novelty relative to the reviewed work, falsifiability,
   feasibility, and decision value. Explain trade-offs with evidence. Do not
   hide uncertainty in a single numeric score; identify candidates that are
   duplicates or depend on the same untested assumption.
4. **Refine.** Improve the strongest candidates in response to the critique.
   Narrow claims that exceed the evidence, make variables and outcomes
   operational, and specify the comparator, baseline, metric, and decision
   criteria. Preserve meaningful alternatives when evidence does not resolve
   them. If no candidate survives, state what evidence or scoping choice is
   needed before designing an experiment.
5. **Select.** Recommend the best-supported, most informative candidate for
   the user's goal and explain why it leads. Name any viable runner-up and the
   condition that would make it preferable. Propose the smallest experiment
   that can distinguish the selected hypothesis from its comparator. State
   what results would support, refute, or leave it inconclusive, including
   important limitations and confounders.

## Deliverable

Return a concise design that includes:

- The research question and relevant evidence or evidence gap.
- The generated candidates and their key critiques, in a compact table if there
  is more than one.
- The comparison criteria and important trade-offs.
- The refined selected hypothesis, plus a runner-up when useful.
- A minimal experiment plan: intervention, comparator or baseline, population
  or data, outcomes and metrics, decision criteria, and likely confounders.
- Supporting citations beside claims, and a clear distinction between facts,
  interpretations, and unresolved assumptions.
- For a proposed Hiraku experiment record, a suggested title, concise summary,
  hypothesis, and change/plan description containing the comparator, metric,
  and decision criteria.

Do not invent citations or imply that an unreviewed field is novel. If source
discovery or evidence access is unavailable, say so and label the design
provisional. Do not implement code, launch jobs, or modify project records.
